C

B

Lena has committed a catastrophic misdiagnosis of TRM. Yes, Marcus is a senior engineer overall, but what is his task-relevant maturity for cross-departmental stakeholder alignment and migration leadership?

It's zero. He's literally never done it before in his life.

B

Exactly. Lena applied a high TRM management style to a low TRM task. Her hands-off approach was appropriate for his pipeline work, but for this new project, it is pure abandonment.

C

He is drowning in the deep end, so he swims back to the shallow end.

B

He thought he was doing fine.

B

Senior people see right through the feedback sandwich. It makes them instantly discount the praise and distrust the manager. Marcus leaves the review furious. He feels set up to fail. B

I gave you autonomy on a task you had never done before. The no surprises rule starts today. A cardinal rule of management. If an employee is startled by critical feedback in a performance review, the manager has failed. So the grand takeaway here is that autonomy is not a blanket status you grant a person. Autonomy is earned and calibrated per task based on their task-relevant maturity. You have to constantly assess, what is my team's TRM for the specific challenge they are facing today? But this introduces a massive logistical problem for the manager. If you aren't micromanaging, how do you know if someone is quietly drowning?

Andy Grove was emphatic about this.

A

If a manager spends 90 minutes in a deeply focused one-on-one with an employee, that meeting can clarify direction, remove blockers, and enhance the quality of that employee's work for the next two weeks. You are improving 80 plus hours of output with a 90-minute investment.

A status update can, and should, be written down in an email or a dashboard. If a manager uses the meeting for status extraction, a dangerous dynamic forms. The employee will stop bringing up real messy problems. They will start bringing you performances. They will curate their updates to assure you everything is fine, right up until the moment the project catches fire. The fundamental rule of the one-on-one is that it is not the manager's meeting. It is the subordinate's meeting. They own the time, and they own the agenda. The employee must prepare the agenda and send it in advance. The sheer act of forcing the employee to sit down, survey their own landscape of work, and write out an agenda forces them to prioritize. It helps them spot trouble early. They should bring their wins, their plans, but most importantly, their half-formed worries and interpersonal frictions.

Why does it have to happen at the very end?
